Exploring Your Personal Lens 🧐

Understand how your unique experiences shape your view of the world.

What is a Personal Lens?

Imagine you're scrolling through TikTok or Instagram. You and a friend watch the exact same video. You think it's hilarious, but your friend finds it cringey. Why? You both saw the same thing, but you saw it differently.

That difference is your **personal lens**. It’s the unique filter—made up of your family, culture, friendships, hobbies, and beliefs—that shapes how you see and understand everything. It’s why two people can experience the same event and walk away with two completely different stories.

🤔 Your Turn to Reflect:

Think of a time you and a friend or family member saw the same movie, game, or event and had a totally different reaction. What do you think in your "personal lens" made you see it differently than they did?

Key Concepts

Perspective / Bias 🤔

A perspective is simply a point of view. A bias is when your lens makes you lean for or against something, sometimes unfairly. We all have biases; the key is to recognize them.

Think of it like: Being a huge fan of a sports team. Your perspective is that your team is the best. Your bias makes you more likely to think the referee made a bad call against your team, even if it was fair.

🤔 Your Turn to Reflect:

Important Vocabulary

  • Culture: The shared customs, arts, and beliefs of a group.
    Example: The music, food, and holidays your family celebrates.
  • Social Group: People you interact with who share similar interests or characteristics.
    Example: Your friend group, a sports team, or an online gaming community.
  • Interpretation: How you understand or make sense of information.
    Example: Your friend texts "k." Do you interpret it as them being busy or mad? Your lens (your relationship with them) affects your interpretation.
  • Empathy: The ability to understand and share the feelings of another.
    Example: Realizing why a friend is upset about something that wouldn't bother you, because you understand their personal lens.

Seeing Through Different Lenses: An Example

Let's see how personal lenses work in a situation you might actually encounter.

The New School Rule 📱

Imagine the school announces a new "no phones in class" policy. Three students—Liam, Sofia, and Chloe—react differently.

  • Liam's Lens: Liam gets good grades but struggles to focus. He sees his phone as a major distraction.
  • Sofia's Lens: Sofia's mom works two jobs and relies on texting to coordinate pickups and family emergencies. Her phone is a lifeline.
  • Chloe's Lens: Chloe is the student body president. She was involved in meetings where teachers complained about phones disrupting lessons.

How do their lenses shape their views?

  • Liam: He's relieved. He sees the rule as a good thing that will help him and others focus on learning.
  • Sofia: She's stressed. She sees the rule as unfair and something that creates a real problem for her family's safety and logistics.
  • Chloe: She feels the rule is necessary. She sees it from the perspective of school leaders and solving a larger problem, even if some students don't like it.

Of the three students (Liam, Sofia, Chloe), whose perspective do you understand the most? Why do you think you connect with their point of view?

Why Your Lens Matters in History Class

Okay, so why are we talking about phone rules? Because understanding your lens is one of the most important skills you can have, especially when studying history. History isn't just a list of dates—it's a collection of stories told through different lenses.

From the School Rule to the American Revolution

Remember how Liam, Sofia, and Chloe saw the "no phone" rule? They all experienced the same event, but their personal circumstances created totally different interpretations—helpful, stressful, or necessary.

Now, apply that to the American Revolution. When we read a textbook, we are reading one interpretation. But what about others?

  • A wealthy Boston merchant (like Chloe, who saw the bigger picture) might see British taxes as a threat to his business and a reason for independence. His lens is focused on economics and self-governance.
  • A poor farmer in the countryside (like Sofia, worried about her family) might not care about taxes but fears a war will destroy his crops and home. His lens is focused on survival and safety.
  • A shopkeeper who is a Loyalist to the British King would see the protests and boycotts as dangerous riots. Their lens, shaped by loyalty to the Crown and fear of chaos, would view the Patriots as illegal traitors who are destroying the peace.

In class, your classmate whose lens values order and rules might understand the Loyalist's point of view, while you might focus on the Patriot's fight for justice. Neither is wrong—you're just looking through different lenses.

The Three Questions of a Good Historian

Thinking like a historian means not just accepting the first story you read. It means being a detective and asking critical questions that are guided by your understanding of personal lenses.

  1. Whose lens am I looking through?

    Is this account from a president, a soldier, a factory worker, a protestor? Knowing who is telling the story is the first step to understanding their perspective and potential biases.

  2. What other lenses are missing?

    History is often written by the winners. For every story that is told, there are often many others that are silent. Who aren't we hearing from? Why might their story be different? Actively searching for these missing voices gives us a fuller, more honest picture of the past.

  3. How does my own lens impact how I view this event?

    This is the hardest but most important question. Do your own values, beliefs, or experiences make you favor one side? Being aware of your own lens helps you stay objective and open-minded, allowing you to analyze history more fairly and accurately.
